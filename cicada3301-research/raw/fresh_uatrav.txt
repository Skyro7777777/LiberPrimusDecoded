 A flyer linked the UofA to one of the internet’s deepest mysteries. 14 years later, it’s still unsolved

In January 2012, a mostly white flyer bearing a QR code and a black, low-resolution image of a cicada was posted near the University of Arkansas’s Mechanical Engineering building. 

The seemingly nondescript poster was actually a key step in an elaborate scavenger hunt, heralded as one of the internet’s greatest mysteries, according to a forum dedicated to chronicling the viral puzzle known as Cicada 3301.

The puzzle began on Jan. 4, 2012, when an anonymous user posted an image on the website 4chan’s infamous /b/ channel, which has been called the “darkest corner of the internet” because of its lack of moderation and frequent lewd, offensive and grotesque posts. According to a 2011 Massachusetts Institute of Technology study, the average life of a thread on the /b/ channel was four minutes. But the image posted on Jan. 4 spawned a community that is still active 14 years later. 

Data inside the image file sent puzzle solvers through a complex series of steps that required knowledge of topics ranging from specialized computer programs to Mayan numerals and obscure Welsh poetry. At one stage of the puzzle, solvers multiplied 3,301 by the pixel dimensions of the original image and added a “.com” to the end. This website showed a countdown beneath an image of a cicada. When the countdown hit zero at 11 a.m. on Jan. 9, 2012, a set of 14 coordinates was posted under the cicada logo along with the message, “Find our symbol at the location nearest you.”

An Arkansas connection

Fayetteville was featured alongside locations such as Paris, France; Seoul, South Korea; and Warsaw, Poland. A forum called Uncovering Cicada Wiki, hosted on the website Fandom, reported that the Fayetteville poster was found but not photographed. The poster’s QR code led to further puzzle stages, but only for a small set of firstcomers. Others were presented with a message reading, “We want the best, not the followers. Thus, the first few there will receive the prize.”

According to documents compiled on Uncovering Cicada Wiki, those who completed the final stages of the puzzle were invited to join the Cicada organization, which, in a leaked email, described itself as a “think tank” advocating liberty, privacy and security and opposing censorship. 

Some details about the Cicada organization could be gleaned from its messages. For example, an X account associated with the organization used the British spelling “organisations” in a 2015 post and typed two spaces after each period, a practice typical of people who learned to type on typewriters. 

A year and a day after the first image was posted, a similar image, verified to be from Cicada, was posted to the same 4chan imageboard. This puzzle followed a similar format and eventually pointed to eight poster locations, including one in Little Rock.

A YouTube video about Cicada 3301 with over 40 million views by the channel LEMMiNO highlighted that no poster was located more than an hour from an airport, so the posters could have been placed by a small number of individuals rather than the local members of a large, multinational organization. 

Emilee Baker, a park interpreter at the Plantation Agriculture Museum in Scott, generally subscribes to the video’s theory.

“The location of the Cicada 3301 poster in LR was at a bus stop near the corner of Markham and Scott, which was, at the time, connected to the airport by the #20 bus,” she wrote in a reply on an Arkansas history discussion group hosted by the University Libraries’ Special Collections. “To me, that makes the Little Rock post fit well within this category of sites that were not chosen for anything specific about the city, but because they were easily accessible from airports. The Fayetteville location isn't quite as direct, but does appear to also be near a bus stop... though I'm assuming it's the campus transit.”

Since the inception of the Cicada puzzles, people working to solve them have collaborated through IRCs, Internet Relay Chats, or more recently, through Discord. CicadaSolvers is one of the most popular Discord servers related to Cicada, with more than 10,000 members. 

Server administrators, who have worked on the puzzles since 2013, said the selection of poster locations was likely just the organization’s attempt to encourage global participation. While there may be an explanation for why two locations in Arkansas were chosen, it is also possible that it is a coincidence. Over the years, people on the server have labored to find meaning in the poster locations but never produced any evidence, they said.

The mystery continues

Cicada resurfaced in 2014 with a puzzle centered around a 58-page book called the “Liber Primus,” which means ‘first book’ in Latin. Written in a runic alphabet, most of its pages are still unsolved and are believed to be stacked with multiple layers of encryption by those who have tried to solve it. No additional puzzles have been released by Cicada after 2014. The last confirmed message from Cicada was posted on a code-sharing website on April 4, 2017, and told those working on the puzzle to “Beware false paths.” 

Server administrators said the unsolved pages of the “Liber Primus” are far more difficult than the solved pages. In the period just after the puzzle was released, many people actively worked on it and shared their findings. The large number of people working together quickly solved puzzle steps that required highly specialized knowledge or methods. As the pages became more difficult and fewer people continued working on them, progress slowed, they said.

Grant Kortfelt, a senior computer science student at Worcester Polytechnic Institute in Massachusetts, joined the CicadaSolvers server in 2019. He said he was attracted to the puzzle’s mystery and “mystique.” 

“There's a lot of similar unsolved codes, but nothing with like an internet organization that puts posters up around the world,” he said. 

Like many other solvers, Kortfelt was stumped on the “Liber Primus.” There is a theory within CicadaSolvers that the Cicada organization made the “Liber Primus” unsolvable to effectively end the puzzle while preserving the air of mystery that made it famous, he said.

Decoding the “Liber Primus” and other parts of Cicada’s puzzles requires extensive knowledge in the field of cryptography, which Tran Phuong, who teaches cryptography at the University of Arkansas at Little Rock, defined as “the way to write in the hidden way.” Kortfelt and other solvers have had to learn cryptography and codebreaking methods in their attempts to decode the book.

A common tool they used was analyzing the frequency that each letter or rune appears. In English, letters like “E” and “T” occur more often than letters like “Q” or “Z.” But their analysis returned something that looked like random noise.

“They [codebreakers] really care about the randomness because they just observe and guess,” Tran said. “So they get really scared about the randomness. The more we can make the string be random, they don't have a clue to play by.”

Although the text of the “Liber Primus” appears completely random at first glance, the CicadaSolvers community noticed that the frequency that two of the same runes appear in a row is lower than what would be expected if the text were actually random.

“We just know at some point in the encryption algorithm, the current rune being encrypted knows the cipher text of the one before it,” Kortfelt said.

This information could be potentially exploited, although the puzzle solvers do not know how to yet, he said. The Enigma code, which the Germans used to encrypt messages during World War II, was broken because of a similar property. In its case, an encrypted letter could never appear as the letter it represented.

“We're really grasping at straws,” Kortfelt said. “Anything goes, like we'll try anything, but also we've exhausted so much that we go to the little things for ideas. Sometimes it just gives you something to try.”

In the 12 years since the “Liber Primus” was released, computer hardware and software have improved significantly, giving codebreakers an edge when it comes to analyzing encrypted messages and guessing solutions. Tran estimated that in the near future, quantum computing technology could break the current commercial encryption standard in just a single day. 

“It's just got to be solved at this point,” Kortfelt said. “I've done everything I can do apart from solving it.”
